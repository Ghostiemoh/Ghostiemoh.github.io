<section class="sec"><div class="fids"><span class="n">01</span><span class="t">The brief</span><span class="r"></span></div>
<h3 class="nh">Are we keeping our promise to answer within 20 seconds?</h3>
<p class="lede">A 20-agent support line came with a simple brief: are we keeping our promise to answer calls within 20 seconds, and if not, which agents are dragging the average down?</p>
<p>The data was one row per agent per day, 1,251 rows in all, about 62 to 63 working days for each of the 20 agents. Every row had the same four fields: calls that came in, calls the agent answered, calls the caller gave up on, and the share of that day's answered calls that were picked up inside the 20-second target.</p></section>
<section class="sec"><div class="fids"><span class="n">02</span><span class="t">Two numbers that sound the same</span><span class="r"></span></div>
<h3 class="nh">92.7% answered is not the same story as 70.9% answered on time</h3>
<p>The first pass gave two headline figures for the whole line. It is tempting to read those as one story, "we are about 90% of the way there." They are not one story. The first number is about whether a call gets handled at all. The second is about how long the caller waits first. A line can answer almost every call and still make most callers wait too long, and that is exactly what was happening here.</p>

<section class="sec"><div class="fids"><span class="n">03</span><span class="t">Splitting it by agent</span><span class="r"></span></div>
<h3 class="nh">Nobody on the team was an outlier</h3>
<p>If slow agents were the cause, the 20-second number should be spread out across the team while the answer rate stayed tight. So I ranked all 20 agents on both. The answer rate barely moved from person to person, a spread of about 2.3 points. The speed-to-answer number was looser, roughly 4.8 points, but still a narrow band. The slowest agent on the 20-second metric was still answering nearly seven in ten calls on time.</p>

<section class="sec"><div class="fids"><span class="n">04</span><span class="t">What actually moved the number</span><span class="r"></span></div>
<h3 class="nh">Call volume, not skill, tracked the slow answers</h3>
<p>The one field that tracked the slow answers was call volume. Incoming calls averaged 198.5 per agent-day, and the days and agents with the worst 20-second figures were the ones taking the most calls. When the queue was deep, everyone's speed slipped together. When it was not, everyone recovered together. That is the signature of a staffing and routing problem, not a skill problem.</p></section>
<section class="sec"><div class="fids"><span class="n">05</span><span class="t">What I recommended</span><span class="r"></span></div>
<h3 class="nh">Fix the schedule before you coach the agents</h3>
<div class="edge"><p><b>Treat the 21.8-point gap as a scheduling question first.</b> Staff or overflow-route the busiest intervals so the queue never gets deep enough to blow the 20-second target. Only look at individual coaching after that, because the answer rate is already even across the team and there is very little individual variation left to fix.</p></div></section>
<section class="sec"><div class="fids"><span class="n">06</span><span class="t">The general point</span><span class="r"></span></div>
<div class="closer"><div class="q">Before you average two metrics in your head</div><p>Check what each one actually counts. "Answered" and "answered in time" share a word and not much else. Here, one was fine and the other was a capacity problem wearing the first one's clothes.</p></div>
